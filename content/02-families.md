# Recognize the pattern

Different tactics can appear on the same page. Naming them helps you explain what happened and choose a response. The labels in this course describe the design; they do not, on their own, establish whether something is unlawful.

## Obstruction

A task becomes unnecessarily difficult through extra steps, limited contact hours, or repeated requests for the same information. Signing up may take a click while cancelling requires several calls.

Look for the actual cancellation instructions. Keep a dated record of your request and any confirmation. If you cannot complete the process, record where it failed so you can describe the problem clearly.

## Forced action

You must agree to something unrelated to finish your intended task. For example, a form may combine a needed account setting with permission to receive marketing.

Look for separate choices or account settings. If the terms do not match what you want, consider whether to continue. Do not assume an extra permission is required simply because a form presents it that way.

## Sneaking

An extra charge, product, or permission appears without a clear, deliberate choice. A pre-selected warranty or an unexpected item in the cart is a common example.

Review the line items and selected boxes. Remove what you do not want, check the revised total, and save a record if there is a discrepancy.

## Interface interference

The layout makes one choice prominent while an alternative is hard to see or understand. “Keep my plan” might be a large button while “Cancel” appears as a faint link.

Read all the available actions. Choose by the wording, rather than the size or color of the button. Keyboard navigation can help you locate controls, but you still need to check what each one does.

## Confirmshaming

The wording tries to make you feel guilty or foolish for declining. A button might say “No, I like wasting money” instead of “No thanks.”

Treat the emotional wording as part of the sales message. It does not change what you need or whether the offer is useful to you.

## Nagging

A site repeatedly asks for the same permission or upgrade after you have declined. Repetition can wear down your original decision.

Check whether you can dismiss the prompt permanently or change the related setting. You do not have to accept an offer to make a browsing session worthwhile.

## Social proofing

A message uses other people’s supposed behavior to push your decision: “62 people are viewing this” or “Everyone is upgrading.” The source or meaning of the number may be unclear.

Compare the price, features, and availability independently. A popularity claim is not evidence that an offer suits you.

## Misdirection

A page draws attention toward a benefit and away from a condition that matters, such as a low introductory price displayed more prominently than the renewal price.

Look beyond the headline. Read the billing terms and final confirmation before deciding. Ask what information would change your choice, then find that information.
