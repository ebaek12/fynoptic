You’re juggling a gallon of milk, a half-charged phone, and the creeping realization that you’ve forgotten your reusable bags—again—when the terminal flashes a question that lasts barely a heartbeat: would you like to round up for charity? It is a nickel this time, a dime next time, sometimes a whole dollar that slides through with the same tap as your latte. The ask feels small because it is small, and yet the system behind that quiet nudge is anything but. Beneath the clean prompt lies a maze of donor-advised funds, state registration rules, platform timelines, brand pledges, payment networks, and public expectations—each one capable of turning your kindness into impact, delay, or, in the worst cases, marketing smoke. Understanding how those pennies move is the difference between fueling a mission and fueling a margin.

The everyday theater of the “Would you like to round up?” prompt

At the register or on a checkout page, a retailer partners with a charity, a payment processor, and increasingly a fundraising platform to capture micro-donations with minimal friction. The totals are not trivial; point-of-sale campaigns in the United States have raised hundreds of millions of dollars each year, with Engage for Good reporting over $605 million in 2020 and more than $749 million in 2022 among the largest programs, and billions cumulatively over the past few decades. The format endures because it matches the tempo of shopping: an ask that takes seconds and scales beautifully when multiplied by millions of transactions. (engageforgood.com) What happens after you tap “Yes” is more complex. Some retailers collect and periodically remit a lump sum directly to a nonprofit. Others route funds through a donor-advised fund intermediary, such as PayPal Giving Fund, which then grants to the intended charity on a set schedule. For enrolled organizations, PayPal Giving Fund typically disburses within roughly 10–45 days; for unenrolled ones, it historically took longer but now targets no later than 45 days after the end of the month following the donation for U.S. grants, with policy updates clarifying timelines. The charity’s tax receipt may show PayPal Giving Fund rather than the brand you supported, because the legal “donor” of record is the intermediary fund. These plumbing details can be invisible to shoppers, but they govern when and how the money arrives. (PayPal)

Who holds the money—and for how long

The custody chain matters. When a retailer simply “passes the hat,” your coin rides along with card settlement and is remitted alongside corporate funds. When a platform is involved, donations may be batched and granted on a periodic schedule. The logic is practical—refund windows have to close, accounting has to reconcile—but it creates a “float” period in which money earmarked for a cause sits with a platform or intermediary. California’s new framework for charitable fundraising platforms, finalized in 2024, forces more transparency into this middle step. The regulations require registration, annual reporting, consent from recipient charities, receipts to donors, and clear timelines for sending out donations. In practice, platforms soliciting on the internet for multiple charities in a year must register; as of June 12, 2024, those rules are in effect, and additional sections, including receipts and time-to-disburse provisions, have specific effective dates and expectations. (California DOJ AG Office) These rules matter for checkout giving because they cover the very services that run round-ups and “add a dollar” prompts online, and in some cases even require commercial co-venturers—businesses that advertise sales tied to donations—to register as platforms if they run large multi-charity promotions across the internet. That is a significant shift in compliance scope and helps squeeze ambiguity out of the delays between your tap and the mission’s bank account. (Greenberg Traurig)

The legal vocabulary retailers—and donors—step into without seeing

In the eyes of most state regulators, a store advertising “we’ll donate X for every Y we sell” is wading into commercial co-venture territory. That phrase is dry, but its obligations are not: written contracts with the charity, state filings in some jurisdictions, and advertising that tells you, specifically, how much money moves per purchase and where it goes. Massachusetts, for example, defines the role and explains the filings a commercial co-venturer must make when it runs charitable sales promotions. New York’s attorney general has urged “best practices” for cause marketing that boil down to: say exactly what you’re doing, how the donation is calculated, and when the charity will see the money. These aren’t niceties. They are there because the line between generosity and promotion gets blurry when the register is involved. (Mass.gov) California’s platform regime adds a parallel category—charitable fundraising platforms and platform charities—that captures internet-based solicitation mechanics. If your round-up, donation slider, or “choose from this list of charities” is being enabled online for Californians, the platform behind it likely has to register, audit its charity list for good standing, and give you a receipt. The Attorney General’s guidance page lays out the forms and the cadence for reporting, and nonprofit law analysts have summarized which sections took effect and when. The upshot is that the path your pennies take is no longer a black box, at least not legally permitted to be one. (California DOJ AG Office)

The psychology of the tiny ask: warm glow meets social pressure

Checkout giving sits at the crossroads of two powerful motives. One is the “warm glow” of helping, a well-documented effect in economics in which people derive utility from the act of giving itself. The other is social pressure—the discomfort of saying “no” when a human or a screen asks you for help in a public place. Field experiments on charitable solicitation show both forces at work, and in some formats, pressure can reduce overall welfare for givers who would have preferred not to be asked at all. That’s not an indictment of checkout charity; it is a reminder that design choices—opt-in prompts, clear information, and the ability to decline gracefully—shape whether a program honors the spirit of giving or leans too hard on the friction of saying no. (OUP Academic)

Fees, “100%,” and the silent arithmetic of micro-gifts

When a terminal promises that “100% of your donation goes to charity,” it is often referencing the net transfer from the fundraising platform to the recipient organization. eBay for Charity, for instance, underwrites donation processing so that PayPal Giving Fund can grant 100% of collected donations to recipients. But “100%” statements live within a perimeter. If a partner site or a different processor is involved, partner fees may apply; if the retailer itself is the collector, card processing on the main purchase may not be charged to the donation line, but the business still bears fees for the overall transaction. Conversely, some donation apps disclose both card processing and platform enablement fees explicitly, with the option for charities or donors to absorb them. The relevant question is not whether any fees exist in the world—they do—but who pays and whether the disclosure at the point of ask matches the reality downstream. (eBay for Charity) The new California rules nod toward this clarity by requiring receipts and standardized information flows, including to donors, about how donations are handled. Even when the law is silent on a specific phrasing, the spirit of the regulation is unmistakable: the donor should not need a law degree to understand the financial pathway of their gift. (Nonprofit Law Blog)

The deduction mirage: why your tax write-off often isn’t there

Most checkout donors never see a tax benefit, not because the gift is invalid but because U.S. tax law only rewards charitable deductions if you itemize, and the vast majority of taxpayers do not. Even if you do itemize, the IRS requires that the donation be to a qualified organization and that you hold a bank record or receipt for cash gifts under $250; larger amounts require more formal acknowledgments. The mechanics of checkout giving can complicate receipts: if your donation goes through an intermediary like a donor-advised fund, your tax record will likely reflect that intermediary, not the ultimate charity you had in mind. None of this reduces the moral value of your nickel; it simply explains why your April 15th return doesn’t notice it. (IRS) A persistent myth is that the store “gets the write-off” for your donation. Tax policy analysts and fact-checkers have debunked this: a retailer cannot deduct a customer’s donation that the retailer merely collects and forwards. The deduction, if any, belongs to the person who made the gift. That truth is comforting, but it also underlines why documentation—and the ability to get it—matters when programs advertise tax-deductibility. (AP News)

When corporate pledges meet customer generosity

The most controversial form of checkout fundraising arises when a brand makes a multi-year pledge to a charity and then invites customers to donate toward that same pledge at the register. Critics argue that, without careful disclosure, donors believe their money is additive rather than offsetting a commitment the company has already publicized. The now-familiar example involved CVS and the American Diabetes Association: lawsuits alleged that customer donations were counted toward CVS’s pledged amount. CVS denied wrongdoing, and a federal court in New York later dismissed challenges to its program; still, the episode reinforced how quickly trust erodes if the math behind public commitments and checkout campaigns is not explained plainly. (Law Street Media) A separate and older set of regulatory actions surrounded PayPal Giving Fund, which settled with a coalition of state regulators several years ago to improve disclosures so donors would better understand where and when their money would be granted. That settlement pushed platforms toward clearer explanations of timelines and charity enrollment status—exactly the kind of fine print that determines whether a checkout donation lands in a month or in a quarter. Recent policy updates from PayPal Giving Fund have continued to specify disbursement windows, including the shortened timeline for unenrolled U.S. charities moving forward. (protectthegoodlife.nebraska.gov)

Why donors say yes, and when they shouldn’t

There is a reason your better angels often win at the register: the cost is tiny, the signal is public, and the “warm glow” arrives instantly. That combination is powerful and, when executed with integrity, wonderfully efficient for charities that could never afford to stand at every checkout line on their own. CharityWatch, a long-time watchdog, describes the tradeoffs candidly: checkout giving provides reach and convenience, but it inserts third parties between donor and charity, which increases the risk of delays and miscommunication if programs are not transparent. The right response is not cynicism; it is calibration. Give when the cause is one you would support anyway, when the disclosure is specific, and when you are not being hurried or guilted into a quick moral performance. (CharityWatch)

How retailers avoid turning benevolence into backlash

A trustworthy checkout program feels like an invitation, not a test of character. It names the beneficiary, states the calculation (“we round to the next dollar and grant the difference”), clarifies who is processing the gift, and tells donors how to get a receipt if they want one. It avoids pre-checked boxes that silently add money, and it never implies that customer donations fulfill the brand’s separate pledges unless that is made explicitly clear. If a donor-advised fund is used, the receipt makes that pathway legible. These aren’t merely best practices; in several states and under California’s platform rules, they are compliance requirements. (Perlman & Perlman)

For nonprofits, micro-donations can be a blessing that requires operational maturity

When a charity’s logo appears on a pin pad, the donations may arrive in batched grants, sometimes without full donor contact information, especially when the intermediary is a donor-advised fund that respects privacy by default. That limits stewardship opportunities. It also creates accounting work: reconciling platform statements, tracking restrictions if any, and monitoring enrollment status so funds are not delayed. California’s “good standing” rules—platforms can only fund charities that are current with regulators—have already nudged nonprofits to keep filings tight. The overhead of doing this is real, but the audience is unprecedented; for many organizations, checkout is a front door they could not have built themselves. (California DOJ AG Office)

The consumer script that reduces regret

If you want to keep saying yes without nourishing anyone’s margins unnecessarily, a quiet personal policy helps. Support the causes you care about directly and intentionally across the year. When a register ask aligns with those causes and the disclosure is clear, treat it as a bonus lane and continue. When the disclosure is vague or the campaign feels like a brand’s obligation masquerading as your generosity, decline with a smile. If you hope to deduct the gift, use a card so you have a bank record and, when feasible, ask or look for a receipt that names the qualified organization—remembering that your receipt might list an intermediary donor-advised fund if that’s the pathway. The point is to decide on your terms while remaining open to serendipity.

The bottom line

Micro-donations at checkout are neither a scam nor a panacea. They are a tool. In the best versions, a nickel buys a child’s lunch a day sooner because a platform disbursed on time and a retailer underwrote the processing cost. In the worst versions, the same nickel becomes a line item in a press release or a placeholder in a clearing account while a lawsuit argues about promises and pledges. The new wave of state rules—especially California’s platform regime—will not eliminate every ambiguity, but they do something important: they turn the plumbing into public infrastructure with registration, receipts, and timelines. That gives your generosity better odds of landing where it was meant to go.

Glossary

  • Checkout charity / point-of-sale fundraising refers to small donations solicited during a retail or online purchase, such as rounding up to the next dollar or adding a fixed amount. It’s popular because the ask is low-friction and scales across millions of transactions; recent reports tally hundreds of millions raised annually in the U.S. among the largest programs. (engageforgood.com)
  • Commercial co-venturer (CCV) is a regulatory term for a for-profit business that advertises a sale or event to the public from which a charitable benefit will flow. CCVs often must execute written contracts with charities and meet state disclosure or filing rules when running donation-tied promotions. (Mass.gov)
  • Charitable fundraising platform / platform charity are categories under California law that cover internet-based services enabling charitable solicitations and the charities that facilitate them. They must register, report, obtain charity consent, provide donor receipts, and send funds on defined timelines. (California DOJ AG Office)
  • Donor-advised fund (DAF) intermediary is an organization—like PayPal Giving Fund—that receives your donation and then grants it to the recipient charity on a schedule. Your receipt typically lists the intermediary as the donee for tax purposes; disbursement windows are documented in platform policies. (PayPal)
  • Warm-glow giving is an economic theory describing the personal satisfaction people derive from the act of giving itself, distinct from pure altruism. It helps explain why micro-donations feel good and convert well. (OUP Academic)
  • Social pressure cost captures the discomfort of declining a request to donate, especially in face-to-face or public settings. Experiments show it measurably affects giving behavior, which is why ethical checkout prompts make declining easy. (Econometrics Laboratory)
  • Tax-deductible charitable contribution is a gift to a qualified organization that a taxpayer can deduct if they itemize. Small checkout gifts are usually deductible in theory but rarely reduce taxes in practice because most filers take the standard deduction. Documentation rules still apply. (IRS)
  • “100% of your donation” claims typically describe the absence of platform or processing fees on the charitable grant itself within a given ecosystem; partner sites or alternative pathways may still involve fees, which should be disclosed. (eBay for Charity)

These best practices are table stakes for trustworthy programs. (Mass.gov)
  • PayPal Giving Fund policies explain the donor-advised flow many checkout systems now use, including distribution windows that have been shortened and clarified.
