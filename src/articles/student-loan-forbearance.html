On paper, forbearance looks like relief. You call your student loan servicer, explain your hardship, and they agree to pause your payments. For a few months—or sometimes years—you owe nothing. But while the pause feels like breathing room, interest does not stop. It continues ticking upward every day, adding to your balance. Once the forbearance ends, the bill is larger, the timeline is longer, and the total cost of your education has quietly ballooned. For millions of borrowers, forbearance is less a lifeline than a trapdoor, turning short-term relief into long-term burden.

What forbearance is—and isn’t

Forbearance is a temporary pause or reduction of student loan payments. It is not forgiveness, and it is not deferment. The key distinction is that in forbearance, interest accrues on all loans—subsidized and unsubsidized alike. General forbearance. Granted at the servicer’s discretion for financial hardship, illness, or other reasons.

Mandatory forbearance. Required in certain cases, such as medical residency or military service.

Borrowers often confuse forbearance with deferment. In deferment, interest on subsidized federal loans may be paused. In forbearance, interest never stops.

The math behind ballooning balances

Consider a borrower with $30,000 in federal student loans at 6% interest. That is $1,800 in annual interest, or about $150 per month.

If they enter forbearance for 12 months:

Payments = $0

Interest added = $1,800

New balance = $31,800

If the borrower capitalizes the interest (adds it to the principal), the balance grows further, and future interest is charged on the higher amount. Over the life of a 10-year loan, that one year of forbearance can add thousands in extra costs.

Why borrowers turn to forbearance

Despite the costs, forbearance remains common. Borrowers turn to it because:

It is quick and easy to request, often approved instantly.

It does not require demonstrating eligibility for income-driven repayment (IDR) or deferment.

It provides immediate relief during job loss, medical emergencies, or transitions.

In other words, forbearance is the path of least resistance. Loan servicers often recommend it because it is simpler than explaining repayment plan alternatives.

The hidden problem: servicer incentives

Servicers—the companies that manage student loans—have been criticized for steering borrowers into forbearance instead of income-driven repayment. Why? Administrative ease. Approving forbearance takes minutes; calculating IDR requires paperwork and annual income verification.

Profit structure. Servicers are paid based on loan volume, not borrower success. Steering into forbearance reduces their costs.

Regulatory scrutiny. Investigations have found that major servicers like Navient pushed borrowers into forbearance even when better options existed.

This systemic steering inflates balances nationwide, creating billions in added interest charges.

Case example: a teacher’s decade of forbearance

A teacher with $45,000 in loans spends most of her 20s and 30s in and out of forbearance during career transitions. Each year, interest adds $2,700. After ten years, she has paid over $20,000 in interest without touching the principal. By her 40s, her balance has grown to $55,000 despite years of trying to stabilize. This is not unusual. The Department of Education has documented borrowers who spent 15 years in serial forbearance, with balances doubling or tripling.

A full-page deep dive: forbearance vs. income-driven repayment

The starkest contrast to forbearance is income-driven repayment (IDR). Forbearance. Payments paused, but interest accrues. Balance grows, payoff delayed.

IDR. Payments tied to income, often as low as $0 for low earners. Interest subsidies may cover unpaid amounts on subsidized loans. After 20–25 years, remaining balance may be forgiven.

The difference is structural. Forbearance pauses responsibility but compounds costs; IDR maintains responsibility but limits payments and may reduce long-term burden. Yet many borrowers choose (or are pushed into) forbearance because it is simpler, even though IDR would serve them better.

The pandemic exception

The COVID-19 student loan payment pause, first enacted in March 2020, changed the rules. During that emergency forbearance, interest was set to 0% and payments were paused for over three years. For the first time, forbearance did not balloon balances. This was an exception, not the rule. The pandemic pause demonstrated that pausing payments without pausing interest is policy choice—not inevitability. Advocates argue it should reshape how hardship relief is structured in the future.

Policy debates and reforms

Critics argue that forbearance is too easy to grant and too costly in the long run. Proposals include:

Caps on serial forbearance. Limiting how many months a borrower can spend in forbearance without enrolling in IDR.

Automatic IDR enrollment. Shifting borrowers into IDR instead of forbearance when payments are unaffordable.

Interest subsidies. Reducing or eliminating interest accrual during hardship periods.

The debate reflects a larger question: should hardship relief trap borrowers in higher balances, or should it help them stabilize without penalty?

The bottom line

Forbearance is marketed as relief, but its structure ensures that balances grow. It buys short-term peace at long-term cost. For borrowers in genuine crisis, it can provide breathing room. But for those with recurring hardship, it is a costly substitute for repayment plans that actually reduce balances. The ballooning effect is not an accident—it is how the system is designed. Until policy changes align relief with sustainability, borrowers will continue to discover that every pause in payment comes at a price.

Glossary

  • Forbearance. A temporary pause or reduction in student loan payments during which interest continues to accrue.
  • Deferment. A loan pause in which interest may not accrue on subsidized federal loans.
  • Capitalization. The process of adding unpaid interest to the loan principal, increasing the balance owed.
  • Income-Driven Repayment (IDR). A repayment plan that ties monthly payments to income and family size, with forgiveness after 20–25 years.
  • Servicer. A company contracted by the Department of Education or private lenders to manage billing, payments, and borrower support.
