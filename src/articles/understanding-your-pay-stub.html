Understanding Your Pay Stub

A pay stub looks like a tidy little receipt for your work: hours, rate, taxes, deductions, and what lands in your account on Friday. But the neatness is a disguise. That single page is where tax law, wage-and-hour rules, benefit elections, court orders, and software settings all collide. A single toggle in a payroll system can convert a bonus from “supplemental wages” at a flat withholding into “regular wages” at your marginal rate. A mislabeled deduction can siphon money for a benefit you never enrolled in. A garnishment can over-pull because someone keyed gross instead of disposable earnings. And sometimes the numbers are fine but the story is not—hours misclassified, overtime excluded, uniforms or cash shortages docked in ways the law doesn’t permit. The difference between quiet loss and a clean fix is literacy. Learn what every line is trying to do, and a pay stub turns from a mysterious slip into a map you can navigate—and, if needed, use as evidence.

What a complete pay stub is actually telling you

Every pay stub is a before-and-after story. “Gross pay” is the promise—the wage or salary you earned this period. “Net pay” is the outcome—what’s left after obligations and elections. The middle is where the meaning lives: mandatory withholdings, voluntary benefits, and one-off adjustments that either reflect your choices or enforce a law. When everything is configured correctly, the lines are mundane. Federal income tax tracks to your W-4; state and local withholding mirror your location; Social Security and Medicare show up as FICA; pre-tax benefits reduce taxable wages on the right lines; post-tax items do not; employer contributions register as value you don’t directly pay. When something is off, it usually shows up first as a pattern break: a deduction description you’ve never seen, a percentage that doesn’t add up, a sudden swing in “taxable wages” that your hours can’t explain. Building the habit of reading the middle—slowly, once per month—turns those anomalies into questions you can ask while the error is small.

Taxes you do see and taxes you don’t: how withholding actually works

Federal income tax withholding is not a flat cut; it’s an approximation of your annual liability applied paycheck by paycheck. Employers follow IRS methods in Publication 15-T and the tables embedded in payroll software. Bonuses and commissions can be handled as “supplemental wages,” which triggers a flat withholding rate (often 22%, and 37% above $1 million) unless the employer chooses to aggregate those wages into regular pay for bracket-based withholding. If your bonus feels “overtaxed,” that’s the flat supplemental method doing its job; your true tax reconciles at filing, and the withholding method doesn’t change the final bill—only your cash flow along the way. The point is not to memorize the tables; it’s to know that a spike on your pay stub after a bonus is a policy choice the IRS allows, not a payroll mistake, and that the definitive reference for the methods is the IRS’s 15-T, not an office rumor. (IRS)

FICA is simpler and easier to check. Social Security is 6.2% of wages until you hit the annual wage base; Medicare is 1.45% on all covered wages, with an extra 0.9% Medicare tax above a set threshold for higher earners. For 2025, the Social Security wage base is $176,100; once year-to-date wages cross that threshold, the 6.2% line should stop for the rest of the year, and you should not see it resume until January wages start the clock again. If Social Security withholding continues after you cross the wage base, flag it immediately; the Social Security Administration publishes the current base and even shows the exact maximum employee contribution ($10,918.20 for 2025). Medicare never caps. These aren’t obscure facts; they’re the easiest errors to spot if you glance at year-to-date totals. (Social Security, IRS)

State and local systems add texture. Some states are bracketed, others flat. Some cities add their own levy. Remote and multistate workers may find withholding following the worksite while they mentally live in the home state. Payroll software usually respects reciprocity agreements, but a change of address or a hybrid schedule can knock the settings sideways. If your state tax line looks wrong after a move or a hybrid-schedule change, assume configuration, not conspiracy, and ask payroll which jurisdiction they have on file; most “wrong state” problems are boring data errors that fix quickly once named.

Benefits, pre-tax vs. post-tax, and the way elections change your “taxable wages”

Where most confusion begins is on lines that feel like choices rather than obligations. A pre-tax medical premium reduces your “federal taxable wages,” so the net hit to your paycheck is smaller than the dollar amount shown. A post-tax deduction does not. Traditional 401(k) contributions lower taxable income but do not reduce Social Security/Medicare wages; Roth 401(k) contributions are post-tax and leave taxable wages unchanged this year while making withdrawals tax-free later. If your pay stub lumps “401(k)” without distinguishing pre-tax vs. Roth, ask for the plan code legend; the letters matter.

The same pre-/post- logic applies to commuter and cafeteria plans. Section 125 elections (health, dependent care, transit) change the tax base first and only later the cash in your account. That’s why two employees with identical gross pay can have different “federal taxable wages” on the stub. The quickest way to sanity-check whether a pre-tax election is being respected is to compare “gross” to “taxable” and confirm that the difference reflects your pre-tax items. If you see a medical premium listed as pre-tax but taxable wages didn’t fall accordingly, something is mis-coded.

Garnishments, levies, and the strict math of “disposable earnings”

When courts or agencies order a garnishment, the payroll math is not negotiable. Under the federal Consumer Credit Protection Act, the maximum that can be garnished from disposable earnings (after legally required deductions) is generally the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or the amount by which disposable earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum wage for that week. Child-support orders run on higher caps—often up to 50% (or more in narrow circumstances) of disposable earnings depending on whether the worker supports another spouse or child. The Department of Labor’s garnishment fact sheet explains the weekly math step by step and reminds employers to scale correctly when pay periods cover more than one week. If you see a percentage applied to gross wages or numbers that ignore the 30× floor, don’t argue policy; point to the statute and the DOL guidance and ask payroll to re-run the calculation using disposable earnings. (Legal Information Institute, DOL)

Illegal or unauthorized deductions: where real money quietly disappears

Most unlawful deductions don’t announce themselves. They show up as “misc,” “equipment,” or “shortage” and feel like housekeeping. In many states, those housekeeping lines are not allowed if they primarily benefit the employer or would drop pay below minimum wage. California, for example, polices both the itemized pay-stub itself—Labor Code § 226 requires detailed statements showing gross, net, hours, rates, period dates, and all deductions—and the underlying deduction practices, with separate rules requiring employers to reimburse necessary business expenses rather than shifting them to workers. New York’s Labor Law § 193 similarly limits deductions to those authorized by law or expressly authorized in writing for the employee’s benefit and sets out regulatory guardrails for notice and consent. If your pay stub shows a deduction that isn’t plainly tied to a benefit you chose or a law you recognize, state law is on your side to demand clarity or reversal. (Justia, CalDIR, NYSenate.gov, Department of Labor)

Wage-and-hour enforcement data gives a sense of scale. The U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division recovered more than $202 million in back wages for nearly 152,000 workers in fiscal year 2024—an average of about $1,333 per worker—across industries from restaurants to healthcare. Broader efforts across federal, state, and local levels recovered more than $1.5 billion in stolen wages between 2021 and 2023, according to analysis by the Economic Policy Institute. Those numbers do not prove your stub is wrong; they prove that checking is rational. (DOL, Economic Policy Institute)

When payroll software, not malice, breaks your check

Sometimes the problem isn’t your employer’s intention—it’s the tooling. The 2021 ransomware attack on Kronos’s cloud payroll/timekeeping platform left thousands of employers scrambling to run manual time and pay for weeks; class-action litigation over the incident led to a 2023 settlement with payments for documented out-of-pocket losses and time responding to the breach. Separate coverage at the time underscored the obvious: when the time/attendance system goes dark, the risk of missed or miscalculated pay rises—even where employers later true-up. If your stub went haywire during a known outage window or after a provider migration, keep every screenshot, note the dates, and look for a catch-up line on the next stub; if the true-up never appears, you have a clean paper trail to escalate. (Baker Botts, cybersecuritydive.com)

Even without a headline-grabbing breach, software configurations cause quiet harm. A new benefit year can flip an election from pre-tax to post-tax if the renewal didn’t stick; a location change can leave your state code stale; an update can reset overtime multipliers. The test is always the same: compare this period’s lines to last period’s, then follow the money forward to see if a promised true-up hit. If the pattern is wrong twice, stop asking chatbots for reassurance and ask for a payroll ticket with a case number.

Two real-world mini-scenarios and how the pay stub solves them

Scenario 1: imagine an hourly worker in retail who notices a $35 “register variance” deduction two pay periods in a row. The manager says it’s company policy to dock shortages. The worker’s net pay is now below what minimum wage would have been for the hours shown. In many jurisdictions, that’s two violations at once: an unauthorized deduction and an effective minimum-wage underpayment. California’s guidance explicitly flags deductions for business losses like shortages and breakage as unlawful where they push wages below minimum wage or lack proper authorization; New York allows only narrowly listed or clearly beneficial deductions with strict written consent formalities. The fix is not to debate policy in the break room; it’s to send HR a brief note citing the state rule, attach the stubs, and ask for reversal and a corrected wage statement. If HR balks, state labor agencies take these complaints seriously, and the stubs are your exhibits A and B. (CalDIR, NYSenate.gov)

Scenario 2: a salaried employee receives a year-end bonus. The pay stub shows a separate “SUPP” line with 22% federal withholding, full FICA, and state tax. The employee panics because the bonus “lost almost a quarter” to federal tax. In truth, nothing was lost; the IRS supplemental-wage method applied the flat 22% withholding allowed by 15-T, a cash-flow choice that cleans up at tax time. The only reason to intervene is if the amount pushed the person over $1 million in supplemental wages (rare), where a 37% mandatory flat rate applies, or if the employer mistakenly aggregated the bonus in a way that caused an unhelpful spike in bracket withholding. The solution is literacy: recognize the method from the code on the stub, decide if you want to adjust your regular W-4 for the year to rebalance, and move on. (IRS)

What to do when the numbers don’t add up—and how to create leverage

Start with your own reconciliation. Read the lines aloud as a story: “My gross is X. My pre-tax benefits are A and B. That leaves taxable wages of Y. Federal withholding is computed off Y using the method derived from my W-4. Social Security is 6.2% until I hit $176,100 this year, Medicare is 1.45% on everything. My post-tax deductions are C and D. That leaves net of Z.” If you can’t tell how a number was computed, ask for the calculation sheet; payroll teams have them. If a deduction is unfamiliar, ask for the authorization document; HR should have it. If a garnishment looks too high, ask which figure they treated as “disposable earnings” and point them to the DOL fact sheet and 15 U.S.C. § 1673. If an itemized statement is missing required elements—gross, net, hours, rates, period dates, deductions—cite your state’s pay-stub statute (California’s § 226 is a clear example) and ask for a corrected statement. If the employer doesn’t fix what’s clearly wrong, wage-and-hour agencies take complaints online, and federal and state pages explain how to file. The fact that WHD recovered more than $202 million in back wages in 2024 (and public-interest groups tally billions in recent years) is your reminder that this is a normal form of self-defense, not a tantrum. (DOL, Justia, Economic Policy Institute)

Bottom line

Your pay stub is not a shrug; it’s a contract mirror. Read it like a story, not a spreadsheet. When the story makes sense, you gain confidence and plan better. When it doesn’t, you have language and law to make it right: IRS methods for withholding, SSA caps for FICA, CCPA garnishment limits, state rules on itemized statements and deductions, and public enforcement as a last resort. The habit is small—five focused minutes each month—but the compound effect is large: fewer leaks, faster fixes, and a steady shift from “hope it’s right” to “I can prove it.”

Glossary (plain-English, right where you need it)

  • Gross pay. The wages or salary you earned this period before any deductions. If hourly, gross equals hours times rates (including overtime multipliers, if any). If salaried, gross reflects the portion of annual salary for the period.
  • Net pay. What lands in your account after all deductions and withholdings. A correct net begins with the right taxable wage base and applies only authorized deductions.
  • Taxable wages. The slice of your gross that federal and state income tax systems apply to. Pre-tax benefits reduce this; post-tax items do not. FICA uses its own definition and, unlike income tax, ignores most pre-tax benefits.
  • FICA. Social Security and Medicare contributions. In 2025, Social Security is 6.2% up to $176,100 in wages; Medicare is 1.45% on all wages plus 0.9% extra above the high-earner threshold. The employer matches Social Security and Medicare; your stub usually shows only your half. (Social Security, IRS)
  • Supplemental wages. Bonuses, commissions, or similar payments that employers may withhold at a flat 22% (or 37% above $1 million), or aggregate with regular wages. The IRS’s Publication 15-T governs the methods; it changes cash flow, not your ultimate tax. (IRS)
  • Disposable earnings. Your pay after legally required deductions (taxes, FICA), used to compute garnishment limits. Federal law caps most garnishments at the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or the excess over 30× the federal minimum wage. Child support and certain debts have different caps. (Legal Information Institute, DOL)
  • Itemized wage statement. A pay stub that lists required details. Some states, notably California, require specific elements and impose penalties for noncompliance because a clear stub is how workers verify lawful pay. (Justia)
  • Unauthorized deduction. A withholding not grounded in law or a valid, informed, written authorization for the employee’s benefit. State laws (e.g., NY Labor Law § 193; California DLSE guidance) restrict what employers may take. (NYSenate.gov, CalDIR)
